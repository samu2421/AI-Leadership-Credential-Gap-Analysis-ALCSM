## 2. One-page annotations — the 7 real papers

### 2.1 Spence (1973) — "Job Market Signaling"

- **Main argument:** In labor markets with information asymmetry, education functions as a costly *signal* that separates high- and low-productivity workers, because acquiring the signal is less costly for the genuinely able.
- **Methodology:** Formal game-theoretic/economic equilibrium modeling (not empirical).
- **Important findings:** Multiple signaling equilibria can exist; signals only work if differential cost holds between types; markets can settle on inefficient equilibria.
- **Theoretical contribution:** Founding text of signaling theory in labor economics; later Nobel-cited (2001).
- **Methodological contribution:** Establishes the formal logic of using observable proxies (credentials) to infer unobservable qualities (competence) — the theoretical spine of your entire ALCSM premise.
- **Connection to thesis:** This is your master justification for treating LinkedIn-visible credentials (degrees, certifications, titles) as *signals* of underlying AI leadership competence, and for framing the "credential gap" as a signaling-market failure (people holding the signal without the underlying quality).
- **ALCSM dimensions supported:** All six, at the framework level — Spence is why a credential-scoring approach is theoretically defensible at all. Most directly: Dimension 1 (Formal Education), Dimension 2 (Certifications).
- **Research gap thesis addresses:** Spence theorizes signaling in general labor markets; he does not address digital/professional-network signaling (LinkedIn), nor AI-specific credentials, nor signal *decay* or *inflation* over time — gaps your thesis fills empirically.
- **Key concept:** Signaling equilibrium; cost-differential condition for informative signals. (Paraphrased — do not quote Spence directly beyond short technical terms.)

### 2.2 Tadelis (2002) — "The Market for Reputations as an Incentive Mechanism"

- **Main argument:** Reputation itself becomes a tradeable asset; once reputations can be bought/sold or transferred, they lose some power as sorting devices, but they still generate "ageless" incentives for effort across a career.
- **Methodology:** Dynamic general-equilibrium formal model with moral hazard and adverse selection.
- **Important findings:** Markets for reputation equalize incentives for young vs. old agents; reputations are imperfect sorting devices once transferable; social surplus can fall when reputation trading is observable.
- **Theoretical contribution:** Extends signaling/reputation theory beyond static credentials into dynamic, career-long incentive structures.
- **Methodological contribution:** Formal treatment of reputation as a *continuously updated* signal rather than a one-time credential — directly useful for justifying why your NLP tool should weight *recency* and *engagement history*, not just static credentials.
- **Connection to thesis:** Supports Dimension 6 (Professional Engagement & Continuous Development) — LinkedIn activity, endorsements, publications, speaking engagements function as an ongoing reputational market, not a one-off credential.
- **ALCSM dimensions supported:** Dimension 6 primarily; secondarily Dimension 4 (Technical Contributions, as visible reputational output).
- **Research gap thesis addresses:** Tadelis models reputation markets abstractly; he does not address professional social-media platforms as the substrate for reputation signaling, nor NLP-based automated reputation scoring — your third contribution operationalizes exactly this.
- **Caution:** This is a pure economics paper with no data on leadership or AI. Use it for the *incentive logic* of continuous engagement, not as evidence about AI leaders specifically.

### 2.3 Stark & Pais (2021) — "Algorithmic Management in the Platform Economy"

- **Main argument:** Platforms manage value-creating activity without direct managerial authority, using algorithmic ratings/rankings as a "twisted," non-circular feedback loop that diffuses accountability — a new organizational form distinct from markets, hierarchies, or networks.
- **Methodology:** Conceptual/theoretical essay, organizational sociology.
- **Important findings:** Algorithmic management creates power asymmetries between platform owners, providers, and users; accountability is structurally "deflected and denied" at each feedback loop.
- **Theoretical contribution:** Positions algorithmic management as a fourth organizational form (alongside Williamson's markets/hierarchies and Powell's networks).
- **Methodological contribution:** None empirical — but conceptually frames the accountability risks of any system (including yours) that scores/ranks people algorithmically.
- **Connection to thesis:** This is your critical/ethical counterweight. Your NLP screening tool *is* an instance of algorithmic management applied to professional evaluation — Stark & Pais gives you the vocabulary to discuss accountability, power asymmetry, and legitimacy risk in your Ethics chapter and Discussion/Limitations.
- **ALCSM dimensions supported:** Not a dimension-specific paper — it's a *framework-level critique* relevant to Task 5 (ethics) and your NLP tool's validity claims (RQ5).
- **Research gap thesis addresses:** Stark & Pais critiques algorithmic management of gig/platform workers; they do not address algorithmic credential-scoring of professionals, nor AI leadership specifically. Your thesis extends "algorithmic management" critique into professional credentialing.
- **Methodological weakness to flag yourself:** Because this paper is about gig-economy platforms (Uber, TaskRabbit-style), citing it as direct precedent for LinkedIn-based professional scoring is an analogical extension, not a direct application — say so explicitly rather than implying continuity.

- **Methodology:** Conceptual/perspective paper synthesizing management science and data-science literatures; not original empirical data collection.
- **Important findings:** Causal reasoning, randomized experimentation, and employee participation are proposed as principles to make HR analytics both efficient and socially legitimate.
- **Theoretical contribution:** Bridges data science and HRM theory; articulates why "big data → ML → AI" hype in HR often fails in practice.
- **Methodological contribution:** The "small data problem" argument is directly relevant to you: any single organization has too few AI-leader hires to train robust supervised models — which is exactly why your 500-profile, cross-organizational dataset design is defensible (you're solving their small-N problem via aggregation).
- **Connection to thesis:** Directly supports your NLP pipeline design decisions (Task on pipeline) and your Ethics documentation (Task 5) — this paper is your best-grounded citation for discussing fairness risk and employee/candidate reactions to algorithmic scoring.
- **ALCSM dimensions supported:** Framework-level — supports the *methodological legitimacy* of NLP-based scoring (RQ5) rather than any one dimension.
- **Research gap thesis addresses:** This paper diagnoses the problem (AI-in-HR often fails) but does not propose or test a scoring instrument. Your ALCSM + validation work is a concrete answer to their "path forward" call.
- **Caveat:** Note the title-page ambiguity — it circulates as an SSRN working paper (abstract=3263878) as well as later in *California Management Review* (2018) under similar title/authors. Verify the exact published citation (journal, volume, page numbers) before your reference list goes final; do not cite the SSRN ID as your final source.

### 2.5 Ng, Leung, Chu & Qiao (2021) — "Conceptualizing AI Literacy: An Exploratory Review"

- **Main argument:** "AI literacy" is an emerging, under-theorized construct; the authors synthesize 30 peer-reviewed articles into four components: know & understand, use & apply, evaluate & create, and ethical issues.
- **Methodology:** Exploratory/scoping literature review (qualitative synthesis), not a systematic PRISMA review, not empirical measurement.
- **Important findings:** AI literacy is best modeled as multi-dimensional and adapted from "classic literacy" frameworks (reading/writing analogues); ethical awareness is treated as a core, not peripheral, component.
- **Theoretical contribution:** First attempt at a structured, teachable/assessable definition of AI literacy, grounded in educational-literacy theory.
- **Methodological contribution:** The four-component structure is a ready-made template for operationalizing graded competence — directly transferable to your 0–5 scoring logic.
- **Connection to thesis:** Your strongest source for the *internal structure* of Dimension 1 (Formal Education) and Dimension 6 (Continuous Development) — you can justify scoring levels (e.g., "know/understand" ≈ score 1–2, "use/apply" ≈ score 3, "evaluate/create" ≈ score 4–5) using their framework rather than inventing your own from scratch.
- **ALCSM dimensions supported:** Dimension 1, Dimension 6 primarily; ethics component also informs Dimension 5 (strategic/ethical leadership).
- **Research gap thesis addresses:** Ng et al. theorize AI literacy for learners/educators broadly; they do not address AI *leaders*, credential verification, or professional-network signaling. Your thesis is a domain-specific application (leadership population) of a construct they leave general.
- **Caution — do not overclaim:** This is a *review of a review* situation — Ng et al. themselves synthesized secondary sources; treat their four-component model as a structural scaffold, not as validated psychometric criteria.

### 2.6 Steinbach, Gamache & Johnson (2019) — "Executive Construal-Level Shifts and Flexibility in the Upper Echelons"

- **Main argument:** Executives' strategic choices depend not just on static background traits (the classic upper-echelons view) but on how flexibly they shift their *construal level* (abstract vs. concrete thinking) as situations change — introducing "construal shifts" and "construal flexibility" as new constructs.
- **Methodology:** Conceptual/theory-building paper integrating construal-level theory (Trope & Liberman) with upper-echelons theory (Hambrick & Mason, 1984); illustrated via the acquisition-decision context (not primary data collection).
- **Important findings:** Cognitive flexibility, not just background characteristics, may explain variance in executive strategic effectiveness — addressing what Hambrick called the "black box" between executive profile and strategic choice.
- **Theoretical contribution:** Extends upper-echelons theory beyond demographic/background proxies toward information-processing mechanisms.
- **Methodological contribution:** None empirical, but conceptually justifies *why* credentials/background alone (what LinkedIn shows) might be an incomplete predictor of leadership effectiveness — a useful theoretical caveat for your RQ4.
- **Connection to thesis:** This is your best available substitute for the missing Boyatzis competency-theory citation, but — as flagged in Section 1 — it argues something narrower: that observable executive traits are an incomplete predictor of strategic effectiveness. That's actually a *useful tension* for your Discussion: it lets you argue that ALCSM measures observable/credentialed competence, while acknowledging (via Steinbach et al.) that unobservable cognitive processes may still explain leadership effectiveness variance beyond what any credential audit can capture.
- **ALCSM dimensions supported:** Dimension 5 (Leadership & Strategic Responsibility) — with the explicit caveat above.
- **Research gap thesis addresses:** Steinbach et al. theorize *why* background traits are an incomplete proxy for executive cognition; they do not measure credentials or test this empirically at scale. Your ALCSM + NLP audit is an empirical, large-N test of whether the traits their theory questions actually correlate with anything.
- **Important honesty note:** Do not describe this as "competency theory" in your thesis — it is upper-echelons/construal-level theory, a distinct lineage from Boyatzis-style behavioral competency modeling. Mislabeling it will look like a citation-substitution error to an examiner familiar with the literature.

### 2.7 Raisch & Krakowski (2021) — "AI and Management: The Automation–Augmentation Paradox"

- **Main argument:** Popular AI-management advice wrongly treats automation (machines replace human tasks) and augmentation (humans + machines collaborate) as separable choices where augmentation is simply "better." The authors argue the two are interdependent across time and create a genuine organizational paradox; over-emphasizing either pole produces negative reinforcing cycles.
- **Methodology:** Conceptual paper using paradox theory as an analytical lens, built from critique of three popular AI-management books (not empirical data collection).
- **Important findings:** Organizations that hold automation and augmentation in productive tension (rather than picking one) achieve better complementarities; management scholarship on AI is currently underdeveloped and needs a "substantial change" in how it's conducted.
- **Theoretical contribution:** Introduces paradox theory as the appropriate lens for AI-in-management research, correcting a normative "augmentation-is-always-better" bias in practitioner literature.
- **Methodological contribution:** Explicitly calls for management scholars to get involved in rigorous AI research — a direct legitimation for your thesis's contribution as management-relevant AI scholarship.
- **Connection to thesis:** Frames your core RQ (does credential quality predict AI leadership effectiveness?) inside a live scholarly debate: if effective AI leadership genuinely requires navigating an automation-augmentation paradox, then credentials that only signal technical (automation-side) competence, without demonstrated augmentation/strategic judgment, would be expected to under-predict effectiveness — a testable, literature-grounded hypothesis for your Discussion.
- **ALCSM dimensions supported:** Dimension 5 (Leadership & Strategic Responsibility) and framework-level justification for *why* the RQ matters practically, not just academically.
- **Research gap thesis addresses:** Raisch & Krakowski diagnose a conceptual tension in AI management scholarship; they do not propose any way to measure or screen for the executive competencies needed to manage this paradox. ALCSM operationalizes exactly the gap they leave open.
- **Caution:** This paper is a theory/critique piece with no leadership-credential data whatsoever — resist the temptation to cite it as if it supports specific scoring weights; it supports your *motivation and framing*, not your *measurement model*.
